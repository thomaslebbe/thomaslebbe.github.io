On the Elasticity of Substitution between Clean and Dirty Energy: Reconciling Empirical Estimates and Their Implications for Model Calibration
with Freddy Heylen · UGent FEB Working Paper 26/1140
Estimates of the elasticity of substitution between clean and dirty energy range from below 0.5 to as high as 10. We develop an encompassing specification that nests the low and high estimates and apply it to 13 OECD countries over 1980–2020. The elasticity stays well below one when energy-related technology and infrastructure are held constant. It rises to between 2 and 3 when technology is allowed to respond to prices. Carbon taxes alone are therefore insufficient; investment in clean technology and infrastructure is essential.

The Energy Transition in Long-Run Macroeconomics
2026
How do fossil fuel prices interact with clean energy innovation to drive the energy transition? I build a neoclassical growth model of the United States in which clean technology grows through knowledge spillovers from installed clean capital, supported by public infrastructure. The model determines the whole set of fossil fuel prices and clean innovation rates that form a knife edge. On this knife edge the clean energy share is stable. Away from it, the model shows how the system moves toward a new equilibrium. This explains both the long plateaus and the discrete shifts in the observed clean energy share.

Heat Distributed Unequally in the Sovereign Bond Market
with Azzam Santosa · 2026
We study how heat shocks are priced in sovereign bond markets. Heat shocks differ from disaster risk: their expected losses are small but their variance is large. We first build a sovereign default model, calibrated to the Dominican Republic. In the model, this uncertainty alone widens bond spreads and raises default probabilities. We then validate these predictions in a panel study of 76 countries (1995–2017). The effects are strongest in emerging markets with large agricultural sectors.

Green Fiscal Policy in a High-Debt, Ageing Society
working title
How should governments finance the energy transition when public debt is high and the population is ageing? I build an overlapping generations model with households that differ in income and energy demand. The paper compares financing options and traces who bears the burden across generations.
